# What happens at an NDIS audit: the five kinds, what the auditor reads, and what it costs

**The short answer: an auditor checks that your systems work, not that your folder is neat.** You choose and pay an approved quality auditor from the Commission's list. The auditor reads your documents and samples your records. For a certification audit, they also visit you and talk to your workers and participants.

The NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission tells you which kind of audit you need. Everything below comes from the Commission's own page on audits unless it says otherwise. Where we add what auditors do in practice, from sector webinars, we say so: practice is not a rule.

## The five kinds of audit

| Kind          | Who gets it                                                      | What it is                                                                                                     |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Verification  | Providers who only deliver lower-risk, lower-complexity supports | A desk review of your documents by an approved quality auditor. Nobody visits.                                 |
| Certification | Providers with one or more higher-risk supports                  | Two stages: a desktop audit of your evidence, then an onsite audit within 3 months.                            |
| Mid-term      | Certified providers, at 18 months into the registration period   | A smaller audit of governance and operational management, plus any standard that needed a corrective action.   |
| Condition     | Any registered provider the Commission tells to be audited       | Required when the Commission sets a condition, for example after a provisional audit or a change of ownership. |
| Out of cycle  | A provider who wants to change their registration                | An audit you arrange yourself, for example to add a registration group.                                        |

The Commission's "Initial scope of audit" tells you which kind applies. The general rule is the registration group list: lower-risk groups get verification, any higher-risk group means certification. Our guide [Verification or certification: which NDIS audit do you need?](/guides/which-ndis-audit) has the full list.

## What the auditor reads

For a verification audit, the auditor reads the documents the verification module asks for. The Commission lists them in its required-documentation guide for the verification module. The four areas are human resource management, incident management, complaints management and risk management.

For a certification audit, stage 1 is a desktop audit. The auditor asks for information and evidence against every module that applies to your groups, usually off site.

Stage 2 is the onsite audit. The Commission's own words: the auditor looks at how you are implementing your policies and procedures. That means viewing records, visiting your sites, interviewing staff and participants, and observing supports.

The Commission describes a typical day. The focus moves quickly from what your policies say to what actually happens. You are asked questions like "How do you know participants are safe?" and "Can you show me how incidents are managed from start to finish?"

The auditor wants real examples: incident reports, complaints records, training logs, supervision notes. Some staff files are sampled. The auditor checks worker screening clearances, qualifications, induction records and evidence of ongoing training and supervision.

## The interviews

Participant interviews are a key part of a certification audit. The auditor speaks privately with a few participants and asks simple questions: "Do you feel safe?", "Do staff listen to you?", "Do you know how to make a complaint?"

You must let participants know they are automatically enrolled in the audit unless they opt out. In practice (from sector webinars, not a rule), auditors expect the full list; a provider cannot remove participants from it.

When the auditor observes service delivery, they watch how workers communicate, whether consent is sought, and whether supports match the participant's plan. The Commission's examples are small things: knocking before entering a room, explaining a task before helping, recording the support accurately afterwards.

## The closing meeting and the ratings

At the end there is a closing meeting. The auditor summarises strengths and explains any gaps. A non-conformity does not mean you have failed; it means something needs to be corrected or strengthened, and you are given a timeframe.

Each Practice Standard and quality indicator gets a rating:

- 3: conforms with elements of best practice
- 2: conforms with the NDIS Practice Standards
- 1: minor non-conformity
- 0: major non-conformity

A major non-conformity gives you 3 months to fix the issue, and your registration does not progress until it is fixed. A minor non-conformity gives you longer, and the registration process continues.

The auditor writes the report after the visit and shares a draft with you to check for factual accuracy. The final report goes to the Commission within 14 days of a verification audit, or 28 days of a certification or mid-term audit. The Commission then makes the registration decision.

## How the money works

There is no cost to register with the Commission. You pay the approved quality auditor. The Commission does not set prices for audits; the cost depends on the size and scale of your organisation and the number of participants you support. The Commission encourages you to get quotes from several auditors and compare them.

In practice (sector webinars, 2026, not a rule), a quote is a daily rate times a number of audit days. You can ask for the daily rate and how the days were worked out. Quotes for a sole support coordinator's renewal ranged from about AUD 5,000 to 7,000 in 2026.

Our [cost guide](/guides/ndis-registration-cost) has the ranges we have seen for each audit kind.

One rule that protects you: the Commission's expectations for approved quality auditors (June 2026) say arrangements that compromise impartiality, including referral fees, are not acceptable. Nobody should be paid for sending you to an auditor. Readyline takes no fee from any auditor.

## Getting ready, in the order an auditor works

1. Know your kind of audit and your modules. The free questionnaire shows them with the Commission's text behind each.
2. Have the documents the standards name, adopted and dated, and read them yourself.
3. Keep the registers alive: incidents, complaints, worker checks and policy reviews, with a row for each thing that happened.
4. Keep worker files complete: screening, qualifications, induction, training, supervision.
5. Tell participants about the audit and how to make a complaint. Those are the two things the auditor will ask them.

## FAQ

### Does a verification audit involve a visit?

No. The Commission describes it as a desktop review of the required documentary evidence by an approved quality auditor. The auditor may ask you for more information.

### Can I leave a participant out of the interviews?

Participants are automatically enrolled unless they opt out; you tell them that. In practice auditors expect the full list and may choose not to interview someone who would be distressed, but you do not remove people from it.

### What happens if I get a non-conformity?

A major non-conformity (rating 0) gives you 3 months to fix the issue, and your registration does not progress until you have. A minor non-conformity (rating 1) gives you longer, and the registration process continues.

### How long until the Commission has the report?

Within 14 days of a verification audit, and within 28 days of a certification or mid-term audit. The auditor shares a draft with you first to check the facts.

### Who pays for the mid-term audit?

You do, as with the first audit. It applies to certified providers at 18 months, and the Commission suggests you start preparing 12 months after your registration date.

### What is a condition audit?

An audit the Commission can require during your registration period, for example after a provisional certification audit, when not all classes were witnessed, when minor non-conformities are outstanding, or after a change of ownership.
